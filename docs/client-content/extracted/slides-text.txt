slide5
California Senate Bill 1953 (SB 1953) and the Alfred E. Alquist Hospital Facilities Seismic Safety Act
First required new hospitals built after 1973 to meet stringent seismic safety standards.  After the 1994 Northridge earthquake (which damaged 11 hospitals and caused $3B in hospital damages), SB 1953 was introduced and signed in September of 1994, and is now codified in California Health & Safety Code.  This applies to all facilities with a general acute hospital license but does NOT apply to acute psychiatric hospital licenses.
In this Bill, there are two performance category systems, and building must meet BOTH to be 2030 compliant.
Structural Performance Category (SPC) the building’s bones.
  SPC ratings run 1 through 5.  SPC-1 is “may collapse”; SPC-5 is “fully operational after a major earthquake.  By January 01, 2030, every building used for general acute care services must be a minimum SPC-3.  Building that meet SPC-4D can stay in service indefinitely without further upgrade.
Nonstructural Performance Category (NPC) the guts of the building.
 This covers medical gas piping, emergency power, water supply, HVAC ducting, anchored equipment, elevators, stairs, etc.  By January 01, 2030, every acute care building must be NPC-5, which means the systems can sustain continuous operations for 72 hours after a major earthquake.
There have been numerous Senate Bills pass over the years extending various deadlines.  But the most recent Senate Bills have been vetoed by Governor Newsom.  That leaves hospital facilities with a very difficult choice: (a) figure out how to pay for the necessary projects to comply with the 2030 deadline, or (b) gamble on whether or not the new Governor will sign another extension.

An Owner’s choice of project delivery system and procurement approach strongly influences project results.  These choices are among the first decisions an Owner makes on a project, and they form the foundation of how the project will be developed, procured, executed, and how the key stakeholders communicate and relate to each other.  In making these choices, it is critical for an Owner to consider the specific circumstances of each project, including the procurement options available to the Owner.  After thoroughly considering these issues, an Owner should make a strategic decision as to how to take full advantage of the many benefits that are inherent with each delivery system.  Considerations:
An Owner should conduct a proactive and objective assessment of the unique characteristics of its program / project and its organization before deciding on a particular delivery system.
An Owner should implement a procurement plan that enhances collaboration and other benefits of the delivery system and is in harmony with the reasons that the Owner chose the delivery system.
An Owner using a competitive procurement that seeks a price and technical proposal should: establish a clear evaluation process; ensure that the process is fair, open, and transparent; and value both price and technical aspects in the selection process.
All team members should be educated and trained on the chosen delivery process and be knowledgeable of the differences between it and other delivery systems.
The project team should establish logistics and infrastructure to support the project delivery.
The project team, at the outset, should establish processes to facilitate timely and effecting communication, collaboration, and issue resolution.
